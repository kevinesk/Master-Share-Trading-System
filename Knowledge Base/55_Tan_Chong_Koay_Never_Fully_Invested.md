# Dr. Tan Chong Koay — Never Fully Invest at All Times

> "The mistake most retail and even professional investors make is being 100% invested at all times. Markets have cycles. Cash is a position. The discipline to raise cash near tops and deploy it near bottoms is what separates 50-year survivors from 5-year flameouts."
> — Synthesised from Dr. Tan Chong Koay's *Never Fully Invest at All Times*

Dr. Tan Chong Koay (Founder, Pheim Asset Management) is one of Malaysia's most decorated fund managers. Pheim has managed Asian and Malaysian equity funds through multiple major crises — 1997 Asian Financial Crisis, 2000 dot-com bust, 2008 GFC, 2020 COVID — and consistently outperformed.

His core thesis, captured in the book title: **Never Fully Invest at All Times**. This is institutional asset allocation discipline applied to Malaysian markets, backed by decades of crisis-tested data.

This file extracts the actionable principles for retail and small-fund KLSE traders.

## Part 1 — The Core Thesis

### Why "Never Fully Invest" Matters

Most retail and many institutional investors are 95-100% invested in equities at all times. They reason:
- "Cash earns nothing"
- "You can't time the market"
- "Stocks compound — get fully invested ASAP"

Dr. Tan's counter-argument, backed by Pheim's 30+ year record:
- **Cash IS a position** — its value is opportunity
- **You can time the market — broadly** — not perfectly, but well enough
- **A 30-40% cash buffer preserves capital in 50% drawdowns**, leaving capital to redeploy at bottoms
- **The compound effect of avoiding 1-2 major drawdowns** exceeds the cost of holding cash

### The Math

```
Scenario A: 100% Invested Always
- Bull years: full participation in 12% returns
- Bear years: full exposure to -40% drawdowns
- 30-year compound: ~6.5% annualised (Sharpe ~0.4)

Scenario B: Cycle-Adjusted Cash (Pheim approach)
- Bull years: 80-90% invested, 10-20% cash
- Late cycle: 60-70% invested, 30-40% cash
- Bear years: 40-50% invested, 50-60% cash
- Crisis bottoms: 95-100% invested
- 30-year compound: ~9.5% annualised (Sharpe ~0.8)
```

The Pheim approach generates HIGHER total returns DESPITE holding cash. Why?
- Avoiding -40% drawdowns (recovery from -40% requires +67%, not +40%)
- Deploying cash at -30% to -50% market levels (the cash performs spectacularly when redeployed)
- Compounding consistency

## Part 2 — The Cash Allocation Thresholds

Dr. Tan's framework for determining how much cash to hold:

### The 5 Market Conditions and Allocation Targets

| Market Condition | Equity % | Cash % | Indicators |
|------------------|----------|--------|------------|
| **Crisis Bottom** (rare) | 95-100% | 0-5% | KLCI down 40%+; VIX > 35; capitulation signs |
| **Early Recovery** | 80-90% | 10-20% | KLCI up from bottom, breaking 50-day MA |
| **Mid-Bull** | 70-80% | 20-30% | KLCI in clear uptrend; breadth healthy |
| **Late-Bull** | 60-70% | 30-40% | KLCI making highs but breadth deteriorating |
| **Peak/Distribution** | 40-50% | 50-60% | Distribution days clustering, RS divergences |
| **Bear Confirmation** | 30-40% | 60-70% | KLCI below 200-day MA; sustained foreign selling |

### How to Identify Each Condition

#### Crisis Bottom (95-100% equity)
- KLCI down 40%+ from peak
- VIX > 35 sustained
- Forced selling cascade
- Universal media doom
- BNM emergency rate cuts
- Foreign selling at multi-year extremes
- VERY rare — once per decade typically

**Action**: This is the GIFT moment. Deploy aggressively into quality leaders. You don't get many of these.

#### Early Recovery (80-90% equity)
- KLCI 5-15% above the bottom
- Breaking 50-day moving average
- McClellan Oscillator crossing above zero
- Some breadth thrust signals
- Foreign net buying returning

**Action**: Continue heavy equity exposure. Bucket A momentum maximum (see [DUAL_STYLE_PLAYBOOK](DUAL_STYLE_PLAYBOOK.md)).

#### Mid-Bull (70-80% equity)
- KLCI in confirmed uptrend (above 50-day and 200-day)
- Healthy breadth
- Normal volatility (VIX 15-20)
- Earnings season constructive

**Action**: Standard execution; bring cash to 20-30% for next opportunity. Disciplined position sizing.

#### Late-Bull (60-70% equity)
- KLCI continuing higher but breadth narrowing
- Some distribution days appearing
- Valuations stretched (PE 18-22× market average)
- "This time is different" appearing

**Action**: Take profits incrementally. Raise cash to 30-40%. Tighten stops aggressively.

#### Peak/Distribution (40-50% equity)
- Distribution days clustering (5+ in 25 sessions)
- RS line vs index turning negative
- Breadth deteriorating despite index highs
- Foreign net selling persistent
- Yield curve flat or inverted

**Action**: AGGRESSIVELY reduce equity. Cash to 50-60%. Hold only highest-conviction names. No new buys.

#### Bear Confirmation (30-40% equity)
- KLCI broken below 200-day moving average
- 20%+ decline from peak
- Sustained foreign selling
- BNM cutting rates (acknowledging recession)

**Action**: Defensive only — Bucket B yield names. Cash for redeployment.

## Part 3 — The Crisis Case Studies

Dr. Tan's most powerful evidence — actual Pheim performance through KLSE crises:

### Case Study 1: The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis

**Timeline**:
- 1996: KLCI at 1271 (peak euphoria)
- July 1997: Thai baht devaluation begins regional crisis
- Aug-Sep 1997: KLCI begins decline
- Q4 1997: Crisis intensifies
- Sep 1998: KLCI bottom at 263 (-79% from peak)
- 1999-2000: Recovery to 1000 levels

**Pheim's positioning**:
- 1996: Already raising cash as valuations stretched
- Pre-crisis: 40-50% cash position
- Crisis: Heavy buying at 400-500 KLCI levels
- 1999: Aggressive long positioning into recovery

**Lesson**:
- Pre-positioning cash BEFORE the crisis is what made the difference
- Buying when "obvious" panic is happening = generational opportunity
- Pheim's funds outperformed peers by 20-30% during this period

### Case Study 2: The 2000 Dot-Com Bust

**Timeline**:
- 1999-Q1 2000: Tech mania globally
- Q2-Q4 2000: NASDAQ crash; spillover to KLSE
- 2001: KLSE bottoms

**Pheim's positioning**:
- 1999: Skeptical of tech euphoria
- Pre-crash: Higher cash levels (30-40%)
- During crash: Continued conservative; not catching falling knives
- Post-crash: Selective deployment into damaged but quality names

**Lesson**:
- Not every crash is a "must buy" — some industries are permanently impaired
- Quality matters more than discount
- Patience pays during regime changes

### Case Study 3: The 2008 Global Financial Crisis

**Timeline**:
- 2007: Pre-crisis euphoria (KLCI 1500+)
- Sep 2008: Lehman collapse, global panic
- Oct 2008: KLCI bottom near 800 (-45% from peak)
- 2009: Coordinated central bank response → V-shaped recovery
- 2010: KLCI back to pre-crisis levels

**Pheim's positioning**:
- Mid-2008: Cash raised to 40%+
- Oct-Nov 2008: Aggressive buying at the lows
- 2009: Heavy long positioning
- Result: Pheim recovery far exceeded broader market

**Lesson**:
- The fastest crashes have the fastest recoveries when central banks respond aggressively
- Having cash ready for the bottom is the entire game
- KLSE crisis lows are often shorter than retail expects (weeks, not months)

### Case Study 4: The 2020 COVID Crash

**Timeline**:
- Jan 2020: KLCI at 1611 (relatively stable)
- Feb-Mar 2020: COVID realisation; rapid sell-off
- Mar 19, 2020: KLCI bottom at 1207 (-25%)
- Apr 2020: Aggressive recovery begins
- Aug 2020: KLCI back to pre-COVID levels

**Pheim's positioning**:
- Pre-COVID: Cautious due to elevated valuations
- During crash: Aggressive buying despite uncertainty
- Recovery: Heavy long positioning
- Result: Outperformed market significantly in 2020-2021

**Lesson**:
- The crash was fast but predictable when central banks responded
- "This time is different" applies to causes (pandemic) but not market mechanics
- Quality businesses recover regardless of crash type

### The Pattern Across All 4 Crises

```
Step 1: Pre-crisis valuations stretched → reduce equity proactively
Step 2: Crisis unfolds → cash provides opportunity, not loss
Step 3: At extreme fear (-30% to -50%) → aggressive deployment
Step 4: Recovery → ride the rebound from advantageous position
Step 5: New cycle peaks → repeat
```

Pheim's edge isn't predicting crises — it's being POSITIONED for them.

## Part 4 — The Tan Chong Koay Allocation Math

### How to Calculate Your Personal Cash Threshold

Dr. Tan's recommendation for retail investors:

```
Step 1: Define your maximum tolerable drawdown
   Example: -20% portfolio drawdown is the maximum

Step 2: Estimate market potential drawdown
   In a normal correction: -10-15%
   In a recession: -25-40%
   In a major crisis: -50%+

Step 3: Calculate required cash buffer
   Required cash = 1 - (Max tolerable / Market potential)
   
   Mid-cycle: 1 - (-20%/-25%) = ~20% cash minimum
   Late-cycle: 1 - (-20%/-40%) = ~50% cash minimum
   Crisis-warning: 1 - (-20%/-60%) = ~67% cash maximum

Step 4: Apply the formula based on market condition
```

### A Practical Allocation Example

Portfolio: RM 100,000

**Mid-Bull (Current allocation 75% equity / 25% cash)**:
- Equity: RM 75,000 across 5-8 positions
- Cash: RM 25,000 (in MM funds, short-term bonds, or savings)

**Late-Bull Transition (raise to 35% cash)**:
- Sell ~RM 10,000 of weakest positions
- New: Equity RM 65,000 / Cash RM 35,000

**Peak Warning (raise to 55% cash)**:
- Continue trimming: Equity RM 45,000 / Cash RM 55,000
- Hold only highest-conviction names

**Crisis Begins (deploy cash strategically)**:
- KLCI down 30% → start deploying cash 30% per leg
- KLCI down 40% → deploy another 30%
- KLCI down 50% → deploy remainder
- Result: Going from 45% equity at peak to 100% equity at trough

This is the cycle-adjusted approach. Cash isn't lost return — it's stored return for redeployment.

## Part 5 — When NOT to Hold Cash (Counter-Intuitive Cases)

Dr. Tan acknowledges times when full investment is appropriate:

### Case 1: At Confirmed Crisis Bottoms
- After -40-50% drawdowns
- Wide-spread capitulation signals
- Central bank emergency easing
- Universal pessimism

This is the EXCEPTION when 95-100% equity is correct.

### Case 2: For Very Young Investors (20s-30s)
- Long time horizon (40+ years)
- Limited principal at risk in dollar terms
- Can absorb full drawdowns
- Should still rebalance annually but can run 90% equity steady-state

### Case 3: For Defined-Period Goals
- "I need to retire in 25 years" → cyclical adjustments matter less
- Long enough horizon that timing matters less than consistency

### When Holding Cash Is WRONG
- Trying to "time the market" perfectly (no one can)
- Holding 50%+ cash for 5+ years waiting for crash (opportunity cost)
- Holding cash because of generic "market seems too high" without specific signals

The framework: hold cash based on QUANTIFIABLE signals (allocation thresholds), not vague feelings.

## Part 6 — The Pheim Stock Selection Philosophy

Beyond asset allocation, Dr. Tan's stock-picking principles:

### The Pheim Quality Criteria

For any stock to enter a Pheim portfolio:

1. **Strong cash flow generation** — operating cash flow > net profit consistently
2. **Low debt** — net debt/equity < 0.5 preferred
3. **Sustainable competitive position** — clear moat
4. **Reasonable valuation** — PE < 20× for most names
5. **Dividend track record** — paying dividends for 5+ years
6. **Management integrity** — clean track record, no governance issues

### The Pheim "Survivable" Test

A useful mental model: "Would this company survive a -50% economic shock?"

If yes → core holding candidate
If maybe → smaller position, tighter monitoring
If no → trade only on momentum, exit quickly

This is fundamentally different from growth-at-all-costs investing. Pheim prioritises SURVIVAL over upside.

### Sector Preferences (Pheim-Style)

Pheim has historically favoured:
- **Banks**: MAYBANK, PBBANK (durable franchises, strong moats)
- **Telcos**: MAXIS, AXIATA (recurring revenue, defensive)
- **Consumer staples**: NESTLE, F&N (brand moats)
- **REITs (selective)**: IGB REIT, Sunway REIT (rate-sensitive but stable)
- **Utilities**: TENAGA, Gas Malaysia (monopoly characteristics)

Pheim has been less active in:
- High-multiple tech (volatility too high)
- Pure cyclicals (timing too critical)
- Story stocks (no historical record)

## Part 7 — The Tan Chong Koay Decision Tree

For any portfolio decision:

```
Q1: What is the current market condition?
   Crisis bottom / Early recovery / Mid-bull / Late-bull / Peak / Bear

Q2: What is the target equity allocation for that condition?
   95% / 80-90% / 70-80% / 60-70% / 40-50% / 30-40%

Q3: What is my current equity allocation?

Q4: What is the gap?
   Need to raise cash (sell) or deploy cash (buy)?

Q5: What is the priority?
   Sell weakest positions first (raising cash)
   Buy strongest opportunities first (deploying cash)

Q6: How aggressively to act?
   Major condition change: Move 60-80% of the way in 1 week
   Minor adjustment: Move 30-50% of the way over 2-4 weeks
```

This is mechanical, not emotional. The conditions define the action.

## Part 8 — Common Mistakes Against Pheim Principles

### Mistake 1: "Cash Doesn't Pay Anything"
The opportunity cost of cash is more than offset by avoiding -30% to -50% drawdowns. Over a decade, the dual-style approach beats fully-invested.

### Mistake 2: Trying to Catch the Exact Bottom
Pheim doesn't time perfectly. They deploy in tranches over weeks at crisis-level discounts. Don't wait for the EXACT bottom — buy at the OBVIOUS bottom.

### Mistake 3: Ignoring Valuation in Mid-Bull
"It can go higher" is true. But valuation matters. PE > 22× for the broad market = late-cycle warning.

### Mistake 4: Selling Too Early
Just because you've raised cash doesn't mean you sell ALL equities. The 30-40% cash is the BUFFER, not full exit.

### Mistake 5: Buying Too Slowly at Bottoms
At -40% to -50% market levels, deploy 50-60% of cash within weeks, not months. The recovery is often faster than expected.

### Mistake 6: Confusing Cash With Investment
Cash is a position, but it should be in money market funds, short-term bonds, or interest-bearing accounts — not zero-interest savings.

### Mistake 7: Holding Cash Forever
The framework requires you to deploy cash at appropriate times. Holding 50% cash for 5 years waiting for crash = opportunity cost compounds badly.
